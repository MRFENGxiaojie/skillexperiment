# Voice Techniques by Voice Type

Specific techniques for each voice type. Use with Mode 3 (Voice Injection): extract the brand's voice from the blueprint or the user's example, then apply the techniques for that voice type consistently.

For every voice type, the same process applies: name the voice → apply its sentence rhythm → apply its vocabulary moves → check every paragraph against the signature below.

---

## 1. Direct & Plain

The voice of someone who says the thing and moves on. No throat-clearing, no padding.

**Sentence length:** short, 8-15 words average. One idea per sentence.

**Vocabulary moves:**
- Use the plainest word: "use" not "utilize", "start" not "initiate"
- Strip intensifiers: cut "very", "really", "extremely"
- Prefer active verbs: "we cut costs" not "costs were reduced"
- No filler phrases: "it's worth noting", "needless to say"

**Syntax techniques:**
- Start sentences with the subject: "We did X." Not "In order to achieve X, it is necessary to..."
- End sentences on the action, not a trailing clause
- Use periods where a comma would normally go

**Example:**
- Generic: "It is important to note that our platform enables teams to streamline their workflows effectively."
- Direct & plain: "Our platform cuts busywork. That's the whole pitch."

**Do / Don't:**
- Do keep short sentences even when the content is complex.
- Don't compress everything into one breathless run-on.

---

## 2. Casual & Conversational

The voice of a smart person talking to a friend. Contractions, first person, loose syntax.

**Sentence length:** varied, 10-25 words. Mix fragments in for emphasis.

**Vocabulary moves:**
- Contractions always: "we're", "you'll", "that's"
- Everyday words over formal ones: "stuff", "a ton of", "figuring out"
- Hedging done honestly, once or twice: "honestly", "I think", "probably"

**Syntax techniques:**
- Start sentences with "And", "But", "So", "Or" for a spoken feel
- End with asides and afterthoughts
- Use "you" constantly — this voice talks to the reader
- Sentence fragments for punch: "So we fixed it. For real this time."

**Example:**
- Generic: "The results were impressive, exceeding our expectations in several areas."
- Casual: "It went way better than we expected. Like, embarrassingly better."

**Do / Don't:**
- Do sound like speech, not like writing that was read aloud.
- Don't sprinkle slang everywhere; the voice is casual, not childish.

---

## 3. Technical / Analyst

The voice of an engineer or analyst. Precision is the personality.

**Sentence length:** medium, 15-30 words. Long sentences are fine when every clause earns its place.

**Vocabulary moves:**
- Exact terms over vague ones: "99.95% uptime" not "really reliable"
- Numbers, units, and measurements where others use adjectives
- Technical vocabulary used correctly, never for decoration

**Syntax techniques:**
- Lead with the qualifier, then the claim: "For workloads under 100 req/sec, latency stays below 40ms."
- Compare against a baseline: "X, versus Y without the change"
- Use parentheses for precise qualifications

**Example:**
- Generic: "Our solution significantly improves performance."
- Technical: "Under the 100 req/sec load test, p95 latency dropped from 220ms to 40ms. That's the difference between a snappy app and a dropped-session rate of 1.8%."

**Do / Don't:**
- Do give the number, the method, and the baseline.
- Don't fake precision: "approximately 3.7" is worse than "around 4".

---

## 4. Irreverent / Edgy

The voice of a brand with opinions and no patience for corporate nonsense. High risk, high reward.

**Sentence length:** short to medium, with deliberate bluntness. Fragments for insults.

**Vocabulary moves:**
- Strong verbs and blunt nouns: "kill", "dump", "garbage"
- Bold claims stated as fact: "Most onboarding is broken."
- Mock the industry's sacred cows: "Best practices are just past failures in suits."

**Syntax techniques:**
- Start with the accusation, explain later
- Use "Look," "Here's the thing," "Honestly?" as sentence openers
- One-line paragraphs for the punchline

**Example:**
- Generic: "There are several strategies companies can employ to improve their onboarding experience."
- Irreverent: "Your onboarding is leaking customers. Here's where the holes are, and no, another checklist isn't going to fix it."

**Do / Don't:**
- Do keep the edge pointed at the problem, not the reader.
- Don't be rude for its own sake; the voice needs substance behind the attitude.

---

## 5. Authoritative / Expert

The voice of someone who has done the work and can say so. Calm confidence, no hype.

**Sentence length:** medium, 12-20 words. Steady rhythm, no fireworks.

**Vocabulary moves:**
- Measured claims with explicit scope: "In our experience", "For teams of this size"
- Verbs of judgment: "we've seen", "the pattern holds", "the evidence points to"
- No superlatives; authority comes from precision, not volume

**Syntax techniques:**
- State the conclusion, then the evidence, then the caveat
- Use "That is" constructions to tighten definitions
- Parallel structure for lists of findings

**Example:**
- Generic: "We are confident that this approach will yield excellent results for your organization."
- Authoritative: "We've run this playbook with forty teams. Thirty-six held the improvement past six months. The four that didn't all shared one thing: they skipped the retraining step."

**Do / Don't:**
- Do let the record speak; cite what you actually know.
- Don't hedge everything into mush — authority requires taking positions.

---

## 6. Storyteller / Narrative

The voice that teaches through scenes. Concrete detail is the argument.

**Sentence length:** varied and long, with rhythm that builds. Short sentences land the scene change.

**Vocabulary moves:**
- Concrete nouns over abstract ones: "the CEO's spreadsheet" not "leadership analytics"
- Time and place markers: "last Tuesday", "at 6pm", "in the parking garage"
- Sensory words where honest: "the dashboard went red"

**Syntax techniques:**
- Open in the middle of the action, not with context
- Use the past tense for narrative beats, present for the lesson
- End scenes with a takeaway line

**Example:**
- Generic: "The team faced significant challenges during the migration."
- Storyteller: "The migration was scheduled for 2am. At 1:58, Dave found the problem: the old database had 4,000 orphan rows nobody knew about. He said the fix would take three hours. It took eleven. We still shipped by Friday."

**Do / Don't:**
- Do make every detail earn its place; specifics pile up fast.
- Don't tell a story and forget the point; the narrative serves the message.

## Choosing Between Voices

If the blueprint doesn't name a voice, infer it from the examples the user provides:

| If the example is... | The voice is probably... |
|---|---|
| Short sentences, plain words, first sentence = main point | Direct & Plain |
| Contractions, asides, "you" throughout | Casual & Conversational |
| Numbers, qualifiers, measured claims | Technical / Analyst |
| Strong opinions, blunt phrasing, industry mockery | Irreverent / Edgy |
| Evidence-backed claims, calm delivery, explicit scope | Authoritative / Expert |
| Scenes, time markers, concrete detail | Storyteller / Narrative |

When in doubt, ask the user for one more example rather than guessing. Guessing a voice and getting it wrong costs more than one clarifying question (see Proactive Triggers in SKILL.md).

## Consistency Rules (all voices)

1. **Pick one voice and hold it.** Mixed voices read as a team of writers, which reads as a corporation.
2. **Rhythm is the fastest tell.** Whatever the sentence-length pattern of the voice, apply it in every paragraph.
3. **Voice beats grammar rules you invented.** If the blueprint's example breaks a rule, the blueprint wins.
4. **Never let voice override accuracy.** An irreverent claim still has to be true; a technical number still has to be real.
