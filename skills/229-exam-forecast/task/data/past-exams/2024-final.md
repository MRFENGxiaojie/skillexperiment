## I. True/False Questions (1 point each, 10 points total; mark √ for true, × for false, no reasons required)

1. An offer can never be withdrawn once issued.
2. If a contract cannot be performed due to causes not attributable to either party, neither party is liable to the other for breach of contract.
3. When a creditor assigns its claim, it must obtain the consent of the debtor.
4. The scope of compensation for culpa in contrahendo liability is limited to the expectation (performance) interest.
5. If force majeure occurs during a period of the debtor's delay in performance, the debtor may claim exemption from all liability.
6. A clause in standard terms that exempts the provider's liability is always invalid.
7. After a contract is rescinded, the unperformed part ceases to be performed, and with respect to the performed part, neither party is entitled to claim restoration to the original state.
8. Changed circumstances constitute a ground for exemption; the party invoking changed circumstances is automatically exempt from compensation.
9. If the agreed liquidated damages are excessively higher than the actual loss, the party concerned may request the court to appropriately reduce them.
10. Contracts in oral form are always invalid.

## III. Case Analysis Questions (20 points each, 40 points total)

### Case 1 (about 300 characters)

Company A and Company B signed an equipment procurement contract, agreeing that A would deliver the equipment by October 1, 2023, B would pay within 30 days after acceptance, and the liquidated damages would be 20% of the contract price. In September 2023, the industry association issued a new standard, and the equipment required modification under the new standard. A believed the modification constituted changed circumstances and demanded a 15% price increase and a 2-month extension of delivery; B believed that the equipment price fluctuation and the standard update were normal commercial risk, refused the price increase, and A then stopped production. B urgently purchased replacement equipment from Company C, paying 8% more in price, and only completed acceptance on December 1. B sued, demanding that A pay liquidated damages and compensate for the price difference loss.

Questions:
1. Can A's claim of changed circumstances succeed? State the reasons and the criteria for judgment. (10 points)
2. If A constitutes breach of contract, can B claim both the liquidated damages and the actual price difference loss at the same time? How is it calculated? (10 points)

### Case 2 (about 280 characters)

Company D purchased office furniture from Company E, and the parties' contract stipulated "quality shall be determined solely by D's unilateral acceptance." After the furniture was delivered, D refused to pay the balance on the grounds of scratches on some desktops, but did not arrange any third-party inspection. E argued that this stipulation was a standard term and grossly unfair, and therefore should be invalid, and demanded that D pay the balance and compensate for delay interest. D, in turn, argued that both parties are commercial entities and the clause was determined through negotiation, so the rules on standard terms do not apply.

Questions:
1. Does this "unilateral acceptance" clause constitute a standard term? How should its validity be determined? (10 points)
2. Does D's refusal to pay the balance constitute breach of contract? State the reasons. (10 points)

## IV. Essay Question (20 points)

Drawing on the differences between culpa in contrahendo liability and liability for breach of contract in terms of constituent elements, attribution principles, and scope of compensation, discuss the dual structure of China's contract remedy system and its institutional rationality.
